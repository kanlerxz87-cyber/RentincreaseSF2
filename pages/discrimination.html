Anti-Discrimination &mdash; One Unit or Both?

Whether to raise one unit or both, under federal, state and local fair housing law.

The question asked “Would it be ok to raise the rent on one tenant’s unit, or would it make more sense to raise both tenants’ units in order to avoid any discrimination lawsuits or inquiries?”

Short answer

Apply one written policy to both units. Do not raise only one. And do not artificially equalise the percentages either. Neither extreme is correct, and the reason matters.

Why raising only one unit is the risky course

Not because a differential is inherently unlawful — it is not — but because it leaves the owner unable to answer the discrimination question. Where two similarly situated tenancies in the same small building are treated differently and the file contains no explanation, the inference a tenant’s advocate draws is that the difference tracks something about the household rather than the tenure.

That is especially dangerous for Unit 1, where the household composition changed in April 2026. California FEHA protects against discrimination based on the protected characteristic of a “family member, friend, roommate, or any other associate” — so the added occupant’s characteristics are legally relevant, and familial status is protected federally as well. An increase that visibly follows a change in who lives in the unit is exactly the fact pattern that invites a complaint, even where the real cause is entirely innocent.

Why equalising the percentages is legally wrong and financially costly

San Francisco does not require two units to receive the same percentage. It requires that each increase be lawful for that tenancy — computed from that tenancy’s anniversary date and its own banked-increase history. Unit 1 is entitled to 3.0% and Unit 2 to 13.1% because their tenure histories differ by six and a half years, not because of anything about the people.

The defensible position: a documented uniform policy

Each rent-controlled unit in this property receives the annual allowable increase published by the San Francisco Rent Board, plus any increases lawfully banked under Administrative Code § 37.3(a)(2) and Rent Board Rules § 4.12, imposed on that unit’s own anniversary date, on the written notice required by Civil Code § 827, subject to a current rent increase license under § 37.15, and subject to a documented retaliation screen under Civil Code § 1942.5.

That policy is neutral on its face, applied to every unit, and produces different numbers only because the underlying tenancy facts differ. It is the answer that survives review by a tenant attorney, because each unit’s computation sheet is a mechanical application of published percentages to documented dates — with no discretion exercised anywhere.

Concrete steps to make this evidentiary rather than rhetorical

  1. Write the policy down and date it before serving any notice.
  2. Prepare a per-unit computation sheet showing each skipped anniversary, the official Info Sheet 571 percentage for that period, the 12-month elapse date, and the arithmetic. Retain the source printout.
  3. Run and document the § 1942.5 retaliation screen for each unit, including a nil return where there is nothing to report.
  4. Serve both units in the same manner, with the same notice template, within the same window — even though the effective dates differ.
  5. Never reference a tenant’s household composition, occupancy change, national origin, language, immigration status, source of income, familial status, age, disability or any protected characteristic in any notice, email or contemporaneous note.
  6. Do not use any pricing software that analyses competitor data — § 37.10C makes that unlawful and gives tenants and tenants’ organisations a private right of action.
  7. If a differential is nonetheless chosen — for example phasing Unit 2 at 10% while Unit 1 takes 3.0% — record the reason at the time. “Household already paying above the ZIP median and 42.1% of ZIP renters are cost-burdened” is a documented, neutral, defensible reason. Silence is not.
What would actually create liability Raising only Unit 1 — the unit whose household composition just changed — while leaving Unit 2 untouched, with no written policy and no computation sheet. On those facts an advocate would argue the increase followed the arrival of a new occupant. That argument would be wrong on the law (Topic 152 makes such an increase unlawful, and it would also be the wrong unit to pick on the arithmetic) but it would be hard to rebut without contemporaneous documentation. The uniform written policy is what makes it rebuttable.

One unit or both? The discrimination question

Whether raising rent on one tenancy and not the other creates fair-housing exposure — and what actually does.

?
The direct answer
No law requires you to raise rent on every unit. What the law forbids is a difference that is because of a protected characteristic. The cleanest way to be certain your difference is not, is to apply one written rule to every tenancy and let each tenancy's own history produce the number. On these facts the difference between the two units comes from six years of uncollected increases, not from who the tenants are.

What the statutes actually prohibit

Federal42 U.S.C. § 3604(b): unlawful to discriminate in the terms, conditions or privileges of a rental because of race, colour, religion, sex, familial status or national origin. Rent is a term or condition [law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604]
State — UnruhCivil Code § 51(b): full and equal accommodations in all business establishments regardless of sex, race, colour, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language or immigration status [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
State — FEHAGov. Code § 12955(a): no discrimination or harassment because of race, colour, religion, sex, gender, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, marital status, national origin, ancestry, familial status, source of income, disability, veteran or military status or genetic information [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
State — interferenceGov. Code § 12955.7: separately unlawful to coerce, intimidate, threaten or interfere with a person's exercise of those rights [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Local — SF Human RightsChapter 12 of the Administrative Code adds local protected classes, including weight and height and source of income. The Rent Board publishes no rent-setting guidance under it; a local HRC opinion should be sought if a question turns on a local-only class.

Note what is not in any of these lists: "tenant who has lived there a long time", "tenant paying below market", "tenant who added a roommate", "tenant in the other unit". Tenure and rent history are not protected characteristics. A lawful difference driven by them is not discrimination.

Why Unit 1 and Unit 2 land on different percentages

The two numbers look very different — 3.0% and 13.1% — which is exactly the pattern that invites an inquiry. The explanation has to be documented before the inquiry, not after:

Both units are offered the same annual increase. The difference is entirely the accumulated history of each tenancy. That is the single sentence that answers a fair-housing inquiry, and it is true on these facts.

The three arguments a tenant-side attorney would actually run

  1. "The increase is retaliatory." Civil Code § 1942.5(a) bars a rent increase within 180 days of a good-faith habitability complaint or an agency complaint, and § 1942.5(d) bars any increase that retaliates for tenant organising [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]. Defence: there is no complaint on file, and the increase is on a fixed statutory schedule rather than in response to anything. Document the date of any tenant complaint so the 180-day window is visible.
  2. "The difference between the units is discriminatory." This is the Unruh/FEHA/FHA route. Defence: the written policy plus the table above. No protected characteristic distinguishes E R and I S from L O and M T that anyone has stated; the difference tracks tenancy start dates.
  3. "The increase is unlawful, so it is void." This is the strongest route, and it is not about discrimination at all — it is § 37.3 compliance [sf.gov/information--sec-373-rent-limitations]. It is also the easiest to defend against, because compliance is mechanical: licence, cap, arithmetic, notice period, itemisation.

What would create real exposure

Raising only the unit with the tenant of a particular national origin Direct evidence of disparate treatment. Unlawful under all three statutes [law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604][leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…][leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Raising the unit with children and not the other Familial-status discrimination — explicit under the federal Act, Unruh and FEHA [hud.gov/helping-americans/fair-housing-act-o…][leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Skipping a unit because the tenant is elderly or disabled Even a favourable difference taken on a protected basis is a term or condition difference. If a genuine accommodation is intended, document it as an accommodation, not as a rent policy.
Raising rent in response to a complaint Retaliation, and separately void [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Raising rent for an added occupant Unlawful increase regardless of the lease [sf.gov/information--charging-additional-occu…]
The recommendation Adopt a one-page written policy: "The annual allowable increase under the Rent Ordinance is offered to every tenancy on its anniversary date, together with any banked amount attributable to that tenancy. The amount is calculated per unit from that unit's rent history and the Rent Board's published table. No other factor is considered." Then follow it. Nothing in the three statutes requires identical outcomes; all three are satisfied by an identical rule applied consistently and documented.

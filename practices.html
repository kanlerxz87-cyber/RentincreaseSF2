Dashboard / Owner practices

How rental increases are actually handled

Institutional owners and individual owners, separated. Only practices an official source actually describes are stated; where no official data exists, it is recorded as a gap rather than estimated.

Read this first — what official sources do and do not say There is no government dataset that says "large owners raise rent by X% and small owners by Y%". The San Francisco Rent Board publishes eviction, buyout and petition statistics [sf.gov/information--rent-board-statistics-an…]; it does not publish a distribution of annual rent increases by owner type. So the honest answer separates two things: what the law requires of everyone identically, and what official sources describe about how large operators behave. Anything beyond that would be invented, and is flagged as a research gap instead.

1. The part that is identical for every owner

For a rent-controlled San Francisco unit, an apartment-owning corporation, a REIT and a two-flat owner are on exactly the same footing. The ordinance makes no distinction by owner size:

The ceiling1.6% for the current period, set by the Board, not by the owner [sf.gov/documents/34344/571_Allowable_Annual_…]
The frequencyOnce per 12 months, on the anniversary date [sf.gov/learn-about-rent-increases-san-franci…]
The licenceHousing Inventory reported and a current rent increase licence — a corporate owner is not exempt, though very small owner-occupied buildings can claim a Homeowner's Exemption from the fee, which is a different thing from the licence [sf.gov/rent-board-housing-inventory]
The notice30 days at 10% or less, 90 days above 10%, itemised components [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…][sf.gov/information--sec-373-rent-limitations]
The consequence of errorNull and void, no matter who the owner is [sf.gov/information--sec-373-rent-limitations]

The practical implication for this file: there is no "corporate method" that would let a small owner lawfully exceed 1.6%, and no small-owner latitude that would let one go above the banked total. The method is fixed by statute.

2. What official sources say about large operators

Two federal actions describe institutional practice directly, and both concern the same thing: using aggregated competitor data to decide the number.

DOJ v. RealPageThe Antitrust Division alleged that large landlords used revenue-management software fed with competitors' non-public data to align rents. The proposed settlement requires RealPage to stop using that data, to remove features that "limited price decreases or aligned pricing between competing users", to stop running market surveys collecting competitively sensitive information, and to accept a court-appointed monitor [justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requir…]
Follow-on decreesProposed consent decrees with large landlords (Cortland, Greystar, LivCor, Willow Bridge and later Pinnacle) bar feeding competitor data into pricing tools and bar sharing non-public data with rivals [justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-reache…]
What this means in San FranciscoIt is independently unlawful here. Section 37.10C prohibits the sale or use of algorithmic devices to set rents or manage occupancy for SF residential units — up to $1,000 per violation, per unit, per month [sf.gov/information--algorithmic-devices-set-…]

So the honest characterisation of institutional practice is not "they raise rent more aggressively and get away with it". It is that large operators historically systematised pricing, the federal government and the City have now both moved against the specific data-sharing mechanism, and the lawful ceiling in San Francisco is the same for them as for a two-unit owner.

3. What individual owners in San Francisco do — and what can be verified

Three behaviours are documented in official material, because each has a rule attached:

  1. They bank increases and take them later, or never. The Rent Board maintains a whole page on it, and banking has no expiry during a tenancy [sf.gov/information--banked-rent-increases]. Unit 2 is the textbook case: six years of small increases that were never taken.
  2. They take partial increases. The Ordinance permits taking less than the maximum; the unused portion is banked [sf.gov/information--banked-rent-increases]. Nothing requires an owner to take 1.6%.
  3. They sometimes fall below the cap and then petition. Where a rent is far below comparable units for extraordinary reasons, a special-circumstances petition exists — but the Board is explicit that a long-standing low rent is not an extraordinary circumstance [sf.gov/information--special-circumstances-pe…].

What cannot be verified: how often individual owners take the annual increase at all. No official figure exists for that. It is recorded as research gap G-01 on the Flagged for Review page rather than guessed at.

4. The techniques an institutional operator would use that you can lawfully copy

These are all drawn from published rules, not from any private playbook:

Take the anniversary every yearTaking 1.6% annually keeps the base rent tracking the cap. Skipping it does not preserve the money — it banks it, and banking is lost if the anniversary date changes before it matures [sf.gov/information--banked-rent-increases]
Keep a rent ledger per unitEvery increase, its effective date, its percentage and whether any part was banked. Without it, the banked total cannot be proved in a petition [sf.gov/report-unlawful-rent-increase-or-requ…]
Serve notice earlyA 90-day deadline is easy to miss and, once missed, moves the effective date instead of shortening the notice [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Itemise the noticeAnnual portion, banked portion, banked base dates. This is the single cheapest way to defeat a petition [sf.gov/information--sec-373-rent-limitations][sf.gov/reports/december-2022/rent-board-rule…]
Write the policy downA one-page written policy — "the annual allowable increase is offered to every tenancy on its anniversary" — is the strongest evidence of even-handedness if a discrimination question is ever raised [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displ…]
Do not use pricing softwareProhibited in San Francisco, and the federal antitrust decrees point the same way [sf.gov/information--algorithmic-devices-set-…][justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requir…]

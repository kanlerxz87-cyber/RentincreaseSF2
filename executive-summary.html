1. Do I, as an individual owner, have to inform the Rent Board and hold a license before notifying tenants? — Yes.

Short answer: yes. San Francisco does not exempt individual property owners. Every owner of a rent-controlled unit — individual, family, LLC, or corporation — must report into the Rent Board Housing Inventory, and a current rent increase license must be on file on the effective date of any annual allowable or banked rent increase. The requirement attaches to the covered unit, not to the owner's corporate form.

RequirementOfficial ruleOfficial source
Report into the Housing Inventory“All owners of residential property are required to report” into the Housing Inventory through the Rent Board Portal, annually by March 1.sf.gov — Housing Inventory
Hold a current rent increase license“A landlord must have a current rent increase license on file with the Rent Board before any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect.” The license is generated automatically once a tenant-occupied unit is reported.sf.gov — Housing Inventory
The license is written into the ordinance§37.15(e): a landlord who has substantially complied with reporting “shall receive a license to impose rent increases”; if not, “the license to impose rent increases shall be temporarily suspended during the period of the landlord's noncompliance.”sf.gov — §37.15
Reporting is what unlocks the increase§37.3(a)(1): “A landlord who has reported the required information about their rental unit to the Rent Board as set forth in Section 37.15 shall have a license to impose annually a rent increase which does not exceed a tenant's base rent by more than 60% of said published increase.”sf.gov — §37.3
What happens without it“A rent increase notice that goes into effect while the unit is unlicensed may be null and void (this may apply for multiple years)” and the tenant “may be entitled to a rent refund.”sf.gov — Noncompliance notice
It is separate from the Rent Board Fee“Reporting into the Rent Board Housing Inventory does not request an exemption of the Rent Board Fee.” For 2026–2027 the fee is $59.00 per dwelling unit, due March 1.sf.gov — Rent Board Fee
No corporate exception existsNo official source located creates a different reporting, license, percentage, or notice rule for corporations versus individual owners.sf.gov — Housing Inventory; sf.gov — About Rent Board
What the license does not do: it is not Rent Board approval of your increase, it does not authorize anything above the allowable or banked amount, it does not have to be attached to the notice, and it does not stop a tenant from filing an unlawful rent increase petition (no filing charge). sf.gov — Housing Inventory

Step by step (full detail on the Rent Board Process & License page):

  1. Confirm the unit is covered by rent control (built on or before June 13, 1979; confirm on the SF Property Information Map). sf.gov — Rental laws
  2. Create an account in the Rent Board Portal and add the property.
  3. Report each tenant-occupied unit into the Housing Inventory by March 1 each year; reporting is under penalty of perjury. sf.gov — Housing Inventory
  4. Confirm the automatic rent increase license in the Portal and check its status and expiry for the year of your effective date. Rent Board Portal
  5. Pay the separate annual Rent Board Fee ($59.00 per dwelling unit for 2026–2027). sf.gov — Rent Board Fee
  6. Establish the base rent and the anniversary date (the date of the last increase, or the lease start if the rent has never been increased). sf.gov — Rent increases
  7. Calculate using the percentage in effect on the effective date; add only properly banked percentages; no compounding, no rounding up. sf.gov — Banked increases
  8. Choose an effective date at least 30 days after service (90 days if the increase exceeds 10%), plus 5 days if mailed. Civ. Code §827
  9. Serve the written notice stating the dollar amount, percentage, effective date, and which portion is banked; keep proof of service. sf.gov — Banked increases
  10. Keep the lease, rent history, license, notice, and calculation on file — a tenant may petition, and there is no filing charge. sf.gov — Tenant petitions
  11. Only if you need more than the allowable or banked amount: file the applicable landlord petition before serving the notice. sf.gov — Landlord petitions
Does the license prevent a challenge? No. It removes the most common ground for a successful challenge — an unlicensed increase can be null and void — but it is not approval, and the Rent Board will still review the calculation, notice, and rent history if a tenant petitions. sf.gov — Noncompliance notice; sf.gov — Tenant petitions

2. Bottom line

  • Annual allowable increase: 1.6% for rent-controlled units effective March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027. sf.gov — Current Rates
  • Unit 1: $3,995 × 1.6% = $63.92 per month; new rent $4,058.92. This is the maximum on an April 1, 2026 effective date if the 1.6% rate applies. sf.gov — formula
  • Unit 2: If no prior increases were imposed and the same tenants remain, the banked total is 11.5% plus the current 1.6% = 13.1%. $3,150 × 13.1% = $412.65; new rent $3,562.65. This is a calculated maximum, not a verified current rent. sf.gov — 571 table; sf.gov — Banked increases
  • Notice: 30 days' written notice for 1.6%; 90 days' written notice for 13.1% because it exceeds 10%. Add 5 days if mailed. sf.gov; Civ. Code §827
  • License: A current rent increase license is required before an annual or banked increase takes effect. sf.gov — Housing Inventory
  • One unit or both: There is no official requirement to raise both units. Each unit has its own base rent and rent history. Applying the same percentage to both is not required and may be unlawful if it exceeds a unit's allowable amount. sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Banked increases
  • Discrimination: Fair housing laws prohibit treating tenants differently because of a protected characteristic. Raising both units is not a legal safe harbor; the key is a legitimate, documented, non-discriminatory reason. sf.gov — Housing discrimination; CRD — Housing; HUD — Report Housing Discrimination
  • Tenant-lawyer review: A tenant lawyer can review the calculation, notice, license, and documentation, but no one can guarantee approval in advance. sf.gov — Rent Board counselor

4. What is "reasonable and normal"?

The official standard for a rent-controlled unit is the Rent Board's annual allowable increase, not a market-rate or "reasonable rent" opinion. For 2026–27, that is 1.6%. sf.gov — Current Rates The annual allowable is intended to cover ordinary operating-cost increases; it is the normal, no-petition increase. sf.gov — Rent increases

Unit 2's 13.1% is not the "normal annual" amount. It is a one-time catch-up of skipped annual allowances (11.5%) plus the current annual allowance (1.6%). It is lawful only if the banked calculation is correct, the license is current, and the 90-day notice is given. sf.gov — Banked increases A tenant-friendly lawyer may scrutinize a seven-year catch-up even if it is technically lawful.

No official definition: Official sources do not define "reasonable" or "normal business practice" beyond the Rent Board formula. A tenant lawyer's approval cannot be guaranteed. sf.gov — Rent Board counselor

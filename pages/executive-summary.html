Executive Summary

The decision in one document: maximum lawful increases, earliest lawful dates, notice deadlines, and the flags that must close first.

Standard of proof Every legal proposition and every number on this page is sourced to an official .gov, legislative, Rent Board or U.S. Census Bureau document. 48 sources are registered in the Source Register. Anything that could not be confirmed is left blank and recorded as one of 24 flags on Flagged for Review. Nothing has been inferred. This is not legal advice.

1. Bottom line

The maximum lawful increase for Unit 1 is 3.0% ($3,995.00 → $4,114.85), earliest effective 2027-02-01 on 30-day notice. For Unit 2 it is 13.1% ($3,150.00 → $3,562.65), earliest effective 2027-06-01 on 90-day notice — and that 13.1% is provisional, because proposed legislation would cap banked increases at 10% (Flag F-16).

Nothing can lawfully be done today. The correct action between now and then is to close the 6 HIGH-severity flags. The full summary is in docs/executive-summary.md; the sections below carry its operative content.

2. What is reasonable and normal — three layers of law

Local: the Rent Ordinance is the operative ceiling

The building is stated to have been built in 1940. SF.gov states that units built on or before June 13, 1979 have both rent control and eviction protection, and directs owners to confirm the date on the SF Property Information Map. That confirmation has not been done — Flag F-04 — because the address was not supplied and is personal information.

A two-unit building cannot escape coverage. SF.gov Topic 019 is explicit:

“A single-family dwelling with a legal in-law unit constitutes a two-unit building and is not exempt from the Ordinance. A single-family dwelling with an illegal in-law unit also constitutes a two-unit building and is not exempt, unless both units are rented together as a single tenancy.”
The current allowable annual increase is 1.6% for increases effective 3/1/2026 through 2/28/2027 — 60% of the CPI-U for the Bay Area, never more than 7%.

Reasonable and normal in ZIP 94122 is therefore not a market question. For these units it is an arithmetic question with a legally fixed answer: the annual allowable percentage plus properly banked prior percentages, applied to base rent, once every twelve months, on the anniversary date, on proper written notice, by a licensed owner. A 1.6% increase is routine. A 13.1% increase is also entirely lawful here — seven years of skipped increases accumulated — but it sits at the outer edge of what the ordinance permits without a petition, requires 90-day notice, and will attract scrutiny.

State: AB 1482 does not apply, but California notice law does

California’s Tenant Protection Act caps increases at 5% + CPI or 10%, whichever is lower — currently 8.8% for San Francisco for 8/1/2026 to 7/31/2027. It does not govern these units. Civil Code § 1947.12(d)(3) exempts housing already subject to local rent control restricting annual increases to less than the state cap, and the SF Rent Board states plainly:

“This does NOT apply for units under the rent increase limitations of the San Francisco Rent Ordinance.”
The counter-intuitive consequence: the state cap is not a safety net here. A 13.1% increase on Unit 2 exceeds the 8.8% state figure and is still lawful, because San Francisco permits banking and the state statute steps aside. Do not reason from 8.8%.

California does supply mandatory notice mechanics: § 827(b)(2) — 30 days where the increase is 10% or less of the lowest rent charged in the prior 12 months; § 827(b)(3)(A) — 90 days where it exceeds 10%, measured on the same combined basis. Service is personal delivery or mail under CCP § 1013; the Rent Board adds 5 days where mailed.

§ 1942.5 is the sharpest state-law risk: a rent increase is barred within 180 days of specified good-faith tenant complaints about tenantability, agency complaints, inspections or citations, and separately barred where made to retaliate for lawful tenant organising. Threatening to report a tenant or their associates to immigration authorities is itself prohibited retaliation. This screen cannot be run from the facts supplied — Flag F-06.

Federal: no rent ceiling, but full anti-discrimination reach

There is no federal statute capping rent increases for unsubsidised private housing. Federal law reaches these increases only through the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. §§ 3601–3619), prohibiting discrimination in nearly all housing because of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status or disability. HUD’s current published guidance prioritises cases with strong evidence of intentional discrimination and withdraws earlier guidance documents.

Do not over-read that. A narrowed federal administrative posture leaves untouched California FEHA (enforced by the California Civil Rights Department), San Francisco local law, and each tenant’s private right of action. California’s protected list is far broader and is the operative exposure.

3. Unit 1 — the added occupant is a trap, and the answer is no

Unit 1’s household changed: three original occupants became two, with one occupant added on 2026-04-01, rent unchanged at $3,995. Two doors that look open are firmly shut.

No market-rate reset. Rent Board Rules § 6.14 permits an unlimited increase on remaining occupants only “when the last original occupant vacates the unit”, and only if a written § 6.14 notice was timely served on each subsequent occupant. Costa-Hawkins (Civ. Code § 1954.53(d)) operates only “when the last original occupant no longer permanently resides in the unit”, and SF.gov adds that “a co-tenant, as opposed to a subtenant, is not subject to such a rent increase, regardless of when the co-tenant moved in.” Tenant A is an original occupant and remains. Neither provision is available.

No increase because someone moved in. SF.gov Topic 152:

“The Rent Ordinance prohibits landlords from charging more rent solely because a tenant has added an additional occupant to an existing tenancy, including a newborn child. This constitutes an unlawful rent increase, even if the lease or rental agreement specifically permits a rent increase for additional occupants.”
Tenants overcharged this way may petition to void the increase and recover refunds. The only lawful route to recover genuinely higher operating costs from added occupancy is an O&M petition or a utility passthrough — both requiring Rent Board approval first.

The open question is timing, not amount. Unit 1’s lease began 2/1/2025 and no increase was ever imposed, so 1.4% became available on 2/1/2026 and was skipped. Under Rules § 4.12 a skipped increase banks only once 12 full months have elapsed from when it could first have been imposed — here, on or after 2/1/2027. Imposing on that date yields banked 1.4% plus the annual 1.6% (still in effect through 2/28/2027) = 3.0%, and preserves the February 1 anniversary date.

Acting early is expensive. Imposing in December 2026 yields only 1.6%, and SF.gov’s own worked example says the skipped 1.4% is then “permanently lost due to the change in anniversary date.” That costs $55.93 per month — $671.16 per year, forever. Waiting roughly three months is worth about 1.9× the immediate option.

Unresolved: whether executing a new written rental agreement with the added occupant on 2026-04-01 reset the anniversary date to April 1. No official SF source addresses this fact pattern. Scenario U1-C is published with every dollar value left blank rather than guessed — Flag F-07. Ask a Rent Board counselor; the service is free (415-252-4600).

4. Unit 2 — seven skipped years and a deadline problem

Unit 2’s tenancy began 6/1/2019 at $3,150 and, on the facts supplied, no increase was ever imposed. Seven anniversary dates passed. Banked percentages are added, never compounded, never rounded up (Rules § 4.12). There is no cap on how much may be banked, back to April 1, 1982, and banked amounts do not expire during a tenancy.

  • Effective on or after 6/1/2027: all seven have had 12 full months to elapse → 13.1% banked, plus the annual percentage for 3/1/2027–2/28/2028, which the Rent Board has not yet published (Flag F-18). Banked component alone: 13.1% × $3,150 = $412.65. The final total is deliberately left blank rather than estimated.
  • Effective before 6/1/2027 (earliest realistic date 2027-02-08, driven by the 90-day notice): 11.5% banked + 1.6% annual = the same 13.1%, but the June 1 anniversary date is permanently lost and the 2026-06-01 1.6% never banks.
Deadline problem. A 13.1% increase requires 90 days’ notice. For an effective date of 2027-02-08 the notice must be served by 2026-11-10 — four days from the date of this analysis. Not realistically achievable. For an effective date of 2027-06-01 the notice must be served by 2027-03-03. That is the workable window.

Recommended: U2-A. Impose on 6/1/2027, preserving the June 1 anniversary and capturing 13.1% banked plus the 2027–28 annual percentage. Serve 90-day written notice after the Rent Board publishes the new percentage (historically December–January) and by 2027-03-03.

Alternative: U2-C, phased. SF.gov confirms a landlord need not impose the maximum: “If only part of the allowable increase is used, the rest is banked.” Imposing exactly 10.0% → $315.00 → $3,465.00 requires only 30-day notice and leaves the remainder intact. Given that Unit 2’s occupants already pay 11.9% above the ZIP median and that 42.1% of renter households in 94122 are cost-burdened at 30%+ of income, a phased approach is materially less likely to provoke a petition, and converts one 90-day event into two ordinary 30-day events.

Two live risks. Flag F-16: reporting dated 2026-09-17 describes supervisors introducing legislation to cap banked increases at 10% annually. That is third-party reporting, not an official source, and no enactment was found in any official Rent Board source reviewed — but if it passes as described it caps Unit 2 directly. Treat 13.1% as provisional. Flag F-08: only the 6/1/2019 lease was supplied. A tenant may petition on Form 516C for review of the entire rent history, and the Rent Board recalculates lawful base rent from all increases since April 1, 1982 — including those imposed by prior owners.

5. The compliance gate that voids everything: the rent increase license

SF Admin. Code § 37.15 requires an owner to report into the Rent Board Housing Inventory at portal.sfrb.org to obtain a rent increase license. SF.gov: “A landlord must have a current rent increase license on file with the Rent Board before any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect.” Rent Board Rules § 13.14:

“Any rent increase under Ordinance Sections 37.3(a)(1)-(2) purporting to be effective while the landlord is unlicensed shall render the entire rent increase null and void.”

Correct arithmetic does not save it. The right percentage, the right anniversary date and the right notice period still produce a void increase if the license was not in effect on the effective date — not the service date. For buildings with fewer than 10 residential units, reporting began March 1, 2023, annually thereafter. A 2-unit building therefore needed submissions for 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026. There is no monetary penalty for filing late and late submissions are accepted, but the increase is unenforceable until the license exists. License status cannot be verified from outside the portal — Flag F-22.

One point of owner self-interest: SF.gov states that Housing Inventory submissions are public, including owner name and business contact information, square footage, bedroom and bathroom counts, occupancy history, and base rent in $250 increments, filed under penalty of perjury. Complying with the licensing precondition necessarily publishes some property-level data through a City system — a City disclosure, not one made by this repository.

## 2. What is "reasonable and normal" here — the three layers of law

### Local: the San Francisco Rent Ordinance is the operative ceiling

The building is stated to have been built in **1940**. SF.gov states that residential units
built on or before **June 13, 1979** have both rent control and eviction protection, and
directs owners to confirm the construction date on the [SF Property Information Map](https://sfplanninggis.org/PIM/).
That confirmation has **not** been done — Flag **F-04** — because the address was not supplied
and is personal information.

Critically, a two-unit building cannot escape coverage. SF.gov Topic 019 is explicit:

> "A single-family dwelling with a legal in-law unit constitutes a two-unit building and is
> **not exempt** from the Ordinance. A single-family dwelling with an illegal in-law unit also
> constitutes a two-unit building and is not exempt, unless both units are rented together as
> a single tenancy."

The current allowable annual increase is **1.6%** for increases effective 3/1/2026 through
2/28/2027, set at 60% of the CPI-U for the Bay Area and never more than 7%. It is confirmed
by three independent official sources.

**What counts as reasonable and normal in ZIP 94122 is therefore not a market question at all.**
For these units it is an arithmetic question with a legally fixed answer: the annual allowable
percentage, plus any properly banked prior percentages, applied to base rent, once every twelve
months, on the anniversary date, on proper written notice, by a licensed owner. A 1.6% increase
is unremarkable and routine. A 13.1% increase is *also* entirely lawful here — because seven
years of skipped increases accumulated — but it is at the outer edge of what the ordinance
permits without a petition, it requires 90-day notice, and it will attract scrutiny.

### State: AB 1482 does not apply, but California notice law does

California's Tenant Protection Act caps increases at 5% + CPI or 10%, whichever is lower —
currently **8.8%** for San Francisco for 8/1/2026 to 7/31/2027. It does **not** govern these
units. Civil Code § 1947.12(d)(3) exempts housing already subject to local rent control that
restricts annual increases to less than the state cap, and the SF Rent Board states plainly:
*"This does NOT apply for units under the rent increase limitations of the San Francisco Rent
Ordinance."*

This has a counter-intuitive consequence the owner must understand: **the state cap is not a
safety net here.** A 13.1% increase on Unit 2 would blow through the 8.8% state figure and
still be lawful, because San Francisco permits banking and the state statute steps aside. Do
not reason from the 8.8% number.

California *does* supply the notice mechanics, and they are mandatory:
**Civil Code § 827(b)(2)** — 30 days' written notice where the increase is 10% or less of the
lowest rent charged in the prior 12 months; **§ 827(b)(3)(A)** — **90 days** where it exceeds
10%, measured on the same combined basis. Service is personal delivery or mail under CCP
§ 1013; the Rent Board adds 5 days where mailed.

**Civil Code § 1942.5** is the sharpest state-law risk. It bars a landlord from increasing
rent within **180 days** of specified good-faith tenant complaints about tenantability, agency
complaints, inspections or citations, and separately bars an increase made to retaliate for
lawful tenant organising. Threatening to report a tenant or their associates to immigration
authorities is itself prohibited retaliation. This screen **cannot be run** from the facts
supplied — Flag **F-06**.

### Federal: no rent ceiling, but full anti-discrimination reach

There is **no federal statute capping rent increases** for unsubsidised private housing.
Federal law reaches these increases only through the **Fair Housing Act** (42 U.S.C. §§ 3601–3619),
which prohibits discrimination in nearly all housing because of race, color, national origin,
religion, sex, familial status or disability. HUD's current published enforcement guidance
prioritises cases with strong evidence of **intentional** discrimination and withdraws earlier
guidance documents.

Do not over-read that. A narrowed *federal administrative* posture leaves untouched:
California FEHA (enforced by the California Civil Rights Department), San Francisco local law,
and each tenant's **private right of action**. In practice California's list is far broader
than the federal one and is the operative exposure.

## 3. Unit 1 — the added occupant is a trap, and the answer is no

Unit 1's household changed: three original occupants became two, with one occupant added on
2026-04-01, and the rent stayed at $3,995. Two doors that look open are firmly shut.

**No market-rate reset.** Rent Board Rules § 6.14 permits an unlimited increase on remaining
occupants only *"when the last original occupant vacates the unit"*, and only if the landlord
timely served a written § 6.14 notice on each subsequent occupant. Costa-Hawkins
(Civ. Code § 1954.53(d)) likewise operates only *"when the last original occupant no longer
permanently resides in the unit"*, and SF.gov adds that *"a co-tenant, as opposed to a
subtenant, is not subject to such a rent increase, regardless of when the co-tenant moved in."*
**Tenant A is an original occupant and remains.** Neither provision is available.

**No increase because someone moved in.** SF.gov Topic 152 is unambiguous:

> "The Rent Ordinance prohibits landlords from charging more rent solely because a tenant has
> added an additional occupant to an existing tenancy, including a newborn child. This
> constitutes an **unlawful rent increase, even if the lease or rental agreement specifically
> permits** a rent increase for additional occupants."

Tenants who were overcharged this way may petition to void the increase **and recover
refunds**. The only lawful route to recover genuinely higher operating costs from added
occupancy is a petition for an operating-and-maintenance increase, or a utility passthrough —
both requiring Rent Board approval first.

**The open question is timing, not amount.** Unit 1's lease began 2/1/2025 and no increase was
ever imposed, so 1.4% became available on 2/1/2026 and was skipped. Under Rules § 4.12 a
skipped increase banks only once **12 full months** have elapsed from when it could first have
been imposed — here, on or after **2/1/2027**. Imposing on that date yields banked 1.4% plus
the annual 1.6% (still in effect through 2/28/2027) = **3.0%**, and preserves the February 1
anniversary date.

Imposing earlier — say December 2026 — yields only **1.6%**, and SF.gov's own worked example
says the skipped 1.4% is then *"permanently lost due to the change in anniversary date."* That
decision costs **$55.93 per month, $671.16 per year, forever.** Patience is worth roughly 1.9×
the immediate option.

**Unresolved:** whether executing a new written rental agreement with the added occupant on
2026-04-01 reset the anniversary date to April 1. **No official SF source addresses this fact
pattern.** Scenario U1-C is published with every dollar value left blank rather than guessed —
Flag **F-07**. Ask a Rent Board counselor; the service is free (415-252-4600).

## 4. Unit 2 — seven skipped years, and a real deadline problem

Unit 2's tenancy began 6/1/2019 at $3,150 and, on the facts supplied, no increase was ever
imposed. Seven anniversary dates passed. Using the official Info Sheet 571 percentages for the
period containing each June 1:

| Anniversary skipped | Period | Official % |
|---|---|---|
| 2020-06-01 | 3/1/20 – 2/28/21 | 1.8% |
| 2021-06-01 | 3/1/21 – 2/28/22 | 0.7% |
| 2022-06-01 | 3/1/22 – 2/28/23 | 2.3% |
| 2023-06-01 | 3/1/23 – 2/29/24 | 3.6% |
| 2024-06-01 | 3/1/24 – 2/28/25 | 1.7% |
| 2025-06-01 | 3/1/25 – 2/28/26 | 1.4% |
| 2026-06-01 | 3/1/26 – 2/28/27 | 1.6% |

**Banked percentages are added, never compounded, and never rounded up** (Rules § 4.12). There
is **no cap** on how much may be banked, back to April 1, 1982, and banked amounts do not
expire during a tenancy.

- Effective **on or after 6/1/2027**: all seven have had 12 full months to elapse → **13.1%
  banked**, plus the annual percentage for 3/1/2027–2/28/2028, which the Rent Board has **not
  yet published** (Flag F-18). Banked component alone: 13.1% × $3,150 = **$412.65**. The final
  total is deliberately left blank rather than estimated.
- Effective **before 6/1/2027** (earliest realistic date 2027-02-08, driven by the 90-day
  notice): 11.5% banked + 1.6% annual = the same **13.1%**, but the 1.6% is reached as the
  *annual* rather than as a *banked* amount, the June 1 anniversary date is **permanently
  lost**, and the 2026-06-01 1.6% never banks.

**Deadline problem:** a 13.1% increase requires **90 days'** notice. For an effective date of
2027-02-08 the notice must be served by **2026-11-10** — four days from the date of this
analysis. Not realistically achievable. For an effective date of 2027-06-01 the notice must be
served by **2027-03-03**. That is the workable window.

**Recommended: U2-A.** Impose on 6/1/2027, preserving the June 1 anniversary and capturing
13.1% banked plus whatever the 2027–28 annual percentage turns out to be. Serve 90-day written
notice on or after the Rent Board publishes the new percentage (historically December–January)
and by 2027-03-03.

**Alternative: U2-C, the phased option.** SF.gov confirms a landlord need not impose the
maximum: *"If only part of the allowable increase is used, the rest is banked."* Imposing
exactly **10.0%** → $315.00 → **$3,465.00** requires only **30-day** notice and leaves the
remainder intact. Given that Unit 2's occupants are already paying 11.9% above the ZIP median
and that 42.1% of renter households in 94122 are cost-burdened at 30%+ of income, a phased
approach is materially less likely to provoke a Rent Board petition, and it converts a single
90-day-notice event into two ordinary 30-day events.

**Two live risks specific to Unit 2:**

1. **Flag F-16 — pending banking legislation.** Reporting dated 2026-09-17 describes SF
   supervisors introducing legislation to cap banked increases at **10% annually**. This is
   third-party reporting, not an official source, and no enactment was found in any official
   Rent Board source reviewed. If it passes as described it would directly cap the 13.1%.
   **Treat 13.1% as provisional** and re-check the official Rent Board news page before serving.
2. **Flag F-08 — the ledger is assumed, not verified.** Only the 6/1/2019 lease was supplied.
   A tenant may petition on **Form 516C** for a review of the entire rent history, and the Rent
   Board recalculates lawful base rent from **all** increases since April 1, 1982 — including
   those imposed by prior owners. If the property changed hands, obtain the prior owner's
   records.

## 5. The compliance gate that voids everything: the rent increase license

This is the most commonly missed requirement in San Francisco and it is **binary**.

SF Admin. Code § 37.15 requires an owner to report into the **Rent Board Housing Inventory** at
[`portal.sfrb.org`](https://portal.sfrb.org/) to obtain a **rent increase license**. SF.gov:
*"A landlord must have a current rent increase license on file with the Rent Board **before**
any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect."*

Rent Board Rules § 13.14:

> "Any rent increase under Ordinance Sections 37.3(a)(1)-(2) purporting to be effective while
> the landlord is unlicensed shall render the **entire rent increase null and void**."

**Correct arithmetic does not save it.** The right percentage, the right anniversary date and
the right notice period all still produce a void increase if the license was not in effect on
the **effective date** — not the service date. For condominiums and buildings with fewer than
10 residential units, reporting began **March 1, 2023**, with an annual deadline of **March 1**
thereafter. A 2-unit building therefore needed submissions for 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026.

There is no monetary penalty for filing late and late submissions are accepted — but the
increase is simply unenforceable until the license exists. **License status cannot be verified
from outside the portal** — Flag **F-22**. Confirm it for both units before anything else.

One further point of owner self-interest: SF.gov states that Housing Inventory submissions are
**public**, including owner name and business contact information, square footage, bedroom and
bathroom counts, occupancy history, and the base rent in $250 increments, filed under penalty
of perjury. Complying with the licensing precondition necessarily publishes some
property-level data through a City system. That is a City disclosure, not one made by this
repository.

## 6. How large corporate owners and individual owners actually handle this

**A limitation stated up front (Flag F-21):** no official government source was found that
quantifies how corporate landlords set increases differently from individual owners in San
Francisco. Any claim of the form "corporations typically raise rents by X%" would be
unsourced, so none is made. What *can* be verified is how the law treats the two differently —
and those differences are substantial.

| Dimension | Large corporate / institutional owner | Individual owner of a 2-unit building |
|---|---|---|
| Costa-Hawkins single-family/condo exemption | **Unavailable** — Civ. Code § 1947.12(d)(5)(A) disqualifies a REIT, a corporation, or an LLC with at least one corporate member | Potentially available, but **not here**: a two-unit building is expressly not exempt |
| AB 1482 two-unit owner-occupied exemption | Unavailable — requires owner occupancy for the entire tenancy | Potentially available if an owner occupies one unit (unverified — Flag F-05); immaterial, because the Rent Ordinance still applies |
| Algorithmic / revenue-management pricing | **Prohibited outright.** Admin. Code § 37.10C, effective 2024-10-14, bans selling *or using* an "algorithmic device" that analyses non-public competitor data to recommend rents or occupancy. Each month is a separate violation; each unit is a separate violation; up to $1,000 per violation plus fees; tenants **and tenants' rights organisations** may sue | Equally prohibited, but the practical exposure is far lower because individual owners rarely license such software |
| Housing Inventory / license | Same obligation | Same obligation — reporting began 3/1/2023 for buildings under 10 units |
| Institutional capability | Dedicated compliance staff, calendared anniversaries, standard notices, counsel on retainer | Typically self-managed; anniversaries are missed, which is exactly how seven years of increases accumulated on Unit 2 |
| Practical consequence | Errors are systematic and visible, and § 37.10C plus organised tenant litigation is aimed squarely at them | Errors are individual and usually curable — but a missed anniversary is worth real money and can be permanently forfeited |

**The honest read:** in San Francisco the corporate playbook of algorithmic, competitor-data-
driven pricing is **illegal**, not merely frowned upon. What separates a large owner from an
individual owner here is not a bigger lawful increase — the percentage is the same 1.6% for
everyone — but **administrative discipline**. The owner who calendars each anniversary date,
files the Housing Inventory every March 1, and serves notices on time captures the annual
increase every year and never has to attempt a 13.1% catch-up. The owner who does not ends up
in exactly the position Unit 2 is in: lawfully entitled to a large increase that is
administratively awkward, requires 90-day notice, and invites a petition.

**For an individual owner, the defensible practice is the boring one:** take the annual
allowable increase every year, on the anniversary date, by 30-day written notice, with the
license current, the arithmetic documented, and a neutral reason on the record. That is normal
business practice, it is easy to defend, and it never reaches the 90-day-notice threshold.

## 7. How to notify tenants, and what reasons are legitimate

### The notice must be

1. **Written.** Oral notice is ineffective (Civ. Code § 827).
2. **Properly served** — personal delivery, or mail under CCP § 1013. Add **5 days** if mailed.
3. **Timed** — 30 days where the total is 10% or less; **90 days** where it exceeds 10%,
   measured against the *lowest* rent charged in the prior 12 months and *combining* any other
   increases in that window.
4. **Complete in content.** SF.gov requires the dollar amount of the increase, the percentage,
   and the effective date. For a banked increase, Rules § 4.12(b) additionally requires
   **which portion is banked** and **the dates on which that banked amount is based**.
5. **Calculated on base rent only** — which includes housing services such as parking and
   storage, and excludes temporary passthroughs and fluctuating charges.
6. **Not rounded up** to the nearest dollar.
7. **Using the percentage in effect on the effective date**, not the date of service.
8. **Backed by a current rent increase license** on that effective date.

Rules § 4.10(b) makes a non-conforming increase **null and void in its entirety**, subject to a
narrow good-faith readjustment where the overage is no more than half of one percent of prior
base rent. Get the notice right; the penalty for getting it wrong is total, not marginal.

### Legitimate, documentable reasons

| Reason | Status | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| The annual allowable increase published by the Rent Board | **Legitimate — and sufficient on its own** | Admin. Code § 37.3(a)(1) |
| Recovering prior years' increases lawfully banked | **Legitimate**, if 12 full months elapsed on each and the banked portion is disclosed | Rules § 4.12; Admin. Code § 37.3(a)(2) |
| Rising operating costs generally | Contextual. The annual increase is *intended* to cover ordinary O&M increases; going beyond it needs a petition | Rent Board Form 530, Schedule B |
| Capital improvements, rehabilitation, energy-conservation work | **Only with prior Rent Board certification** — never self-imposed | Admin. Code § 37.7 |
| Utility, water revenue bond, or general obligation bond passthrough | **Only via the prescribed worksheet/petition**, kept out of base rent and discontinued on schedule | Admin. Code §§ 37.3(a)(5)–(6) |
| "Market rate" / comparable-unit pricing | **Not available during a continuing tenancy.** Vacancy decontrol applies only to an empty unit | Admin. Code § 37.3(d) |
| A tenant added an occupant, or a roommate was replaced | **UNLAWFUL** even if the lease permits it | Admin. Code § 37.3(a)(11)(A); Topic 152 |
| The last original occupant has left and a § 6.14 notice was timely served | Legitimate — an unlimited increase — **but not available on these facts** | Rules § 6.14; Civ. Code § 1954.53(d) |
| Tenant complained, requested repairs, or organised | **Unlawful retaliation** | Civ. Code § 1942.5(a), (d) |
| Any protected characteristic, or that of a roommate or associate | **Unlawful discrimination** | Gov. Code § 12955; 42 U.S.C. § 3604 |

The practical point: **a landlord does not need to justify the annual allowable increase at
all.** It exists by operation of law. Over-explaining invites argument; understating the
banked breakdown voids the notice. State the numbers, the effective date, the banked portion
and its source dates, and nothing else.

## 8. The tenant-lawyer scrutiny test

San Francisco tenants have real, free, well-organised representation. The correct way to
design an increase is to assume it will be read by a competent tenant attorney, and to build
the file so that attorney concludes it was ordinary, lawful business practice.

**What a tenant can do, at no cost, the moment a notice arrives:**

| Route | Effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Form 518 — Tenant Summary Petition** | Fast-track challenge where a notice is unlawful *on its face* and **has not yet taken effect** — it can stop an increase before it starts | Rent Board Forms Center |
| **Form 516C — Unlawful Rent Increase / rent history review** | Voids the increase, recovers overcharges, and recalculates lawful base rent from **all increases since April 1, 1982** | Rent Board Forms Center |
| **Form 516B — Failure to Repair and Maintain** | Available specifically where the tenant *"received a rent increase notice within the last 60 days"* and legally required repairs were not made | Rent Board Forms Center |
| **Form 516A — Decrease in Housing Services** | Rent reduction where services fell without a corresponding rent reduction | Rent Board Forms Center |
| **Form 516D — Passthrough Challenge** | Attacks improper utility, bond or capital-improvement passthroughs | Rent Board Forms Center |
| **Form 525 — Report of Excessive Rent Increase (AB 1482)** | Rent Board acknowledges receipt and informs the landlord of the law; it cannot enforce state law or hold AB 1482 hearings | SF.gov AB 1482 |
| **Free counseling and legal help** | Rent Board counselor 415-252-4600 (free, cannot give legal advice); Eviction Defense Collaborative 415-659-9184 (free legal help, lead organisation in the city's Tenant Right to Counsel program); Housing Rights Committee 415-703-8644; Bay Area Legal Aid Consumer Rights Clinic 415-982-1300 | SF.gov Tenant and Landlord Resources |
| **§ 37.10C private action** | A tenant **or a tenants' rights organisation** may sue over algorithmic pricing: damages, injunction, up to $1,000 per violation, plus attorney's fees; lease terms limiting a prevailing tenant's fees are unenforceable | Admin. Code § 37.10C |
| **Housing Inventory lookup** | Any tenant can search the Rent Board Portal to check whether the landlord's license is current | SF.gov |

**The eight-question test.** A tenant attorney will work through roughly this. Every answer
must be documented *before* the notice goes out, not reconstructed afterwards.

1. **Coverage** — Certificate of occupancy on or before 6/13/1979? Not a condominium? Not a
   single-family dwelling? → *F-04, F-05 open.*
2. **License** — Was a current rent increase license in effect **on the effective date**, with
   Housing Inventory filings for 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026? → *F-22 open. Failure = entire
   increase null and void.*
3. **Anniversary date** — Is at least 12 months since the lease began or the last increase? Is
   the date the one being used actually correct? → *F-07 open for Unit 1.*
4. **Arithmetic** — Correct percentage for the period containing the effective date? Base rent
   only? Added, not compounded? Not rounded up? Banked portion disclosed with its source dates?
   → *Verified here, machine-computed.*
5. **Banking eligibility** — Did 12 full months elapse on each skipped increase? → *F-03: two
   official sources conflict on 12 vs 24 months. Resolve with a counselor.*
6. **Notice** — 30 or 90 days, correctly measured against the lowest rent in the prior 12
   months and combining other increases? +5 if mailed? Properly served under CCP § 1013? →
   *Verified here.*
7. **Retaliation** — Anything within 180 days: repair requests, habitability complaints, DBI
   citations, Rent Board petitions, tenant-association activity? → *F-06 open. This is the most
   common way a perfect calculation still loses.*
8. **Discrimination** — Is the differential between units explained by a written, neutral,
   uniformly applied policy and documented per unit? Or does it track a protected
   characteristic — including the characteristic of a **roommate or associate**, which FEHA
   expressly protects? → *Answered in § 9 below.*

An increase that answers all eight cleanly is, in the language the owner asked for,
**reasonable, normal business practice, non-discriminatory, and lawful.** An increase that
fails question 2 fails completely regardless of the rest.

## 9. One unit or both? — the discrimination question

**Short answer: apply one written policy to both units. Do not raise only one. And do not
artificially equalise the percentages either.**

Neither extreme is correct, and the reason is worth understanding.

**Raising only one unit, with no documented reason, is the risky course.** Not because a
differential is inherently unlawful — it is not — but because it leaves the owner unable to
answer question 8. Where two similarly situated tenancies in the same small building are
treated differently and the file contains no explanation, the inference a tenant's advocate
will draw is that the difference tracks something about the *household* rather than the
*tenure*. That is especially dangerous for Unit 1, where the household composition **changed**
in April 2026. California FEHA protects against discrimination based on the protected
characteristic of a *"family member, friend, roommate, or any other associate"* — so the added
occupant's characteristics are legally relevant, and familial status is protected federally as
well. An increase that visibly follows a change in who lives in the unit is exactly the fact
pattern that invites a complaint, **even where the real cause is entirely innocent.**

**But equalising the percentages is legally wrong and financially costly.** San Francisco does
not require that two units receive the same percentage. It requires that each increase be
lawful *for that tenancy* — computed from that tenancy's anniversary date and its own
banked-increase history. Unit 1 is entitled to 3.0% and Unit 2 to 13.1% **because their tenure
histories differ by six and a half years**, not because of anything about the people. Forcing
Unit 2 down to 3.0% would forfeit 10.1% of lawfully banked entitlement; forcing Unit 1 up to
match Unit 2 would be an **unlawful rent increase** outright, because there is no basis for it.
Both errors are worse than the differential.

**The defensible position is a documented uniform policy:**

> *Each rent-controlled unit in this property receives the annual allowable increase published
> by the San Francisco Rent Board, plus any increases lawfully banked under Administrative Code
> § 37.3(a)(2) and Rent Board Rules § 4.12, imposed on that unit's own anniversary date, on the
> written notice required by Civil Code § 827, subject to a current rent increase license under
> § 37.15, and subject to a documented retaliation screen under Civil Code § 1942.5.*

That policy is **neutral on its face**, **applied to every unit**, and produces different
numbers only because the underlying tenancy facts differ. It is the answer that survives review
by a tenant attorney, because each unit's computation sheet is a mechanical application of
published percentages to documented dates — with no discretion exercised anywhere.

**Concrete steps to make it evidentiary rather than rhetorical:**

1. Write the policy down and date it **before** serving any notice.
2. Prepare a per-unit computation sheet showing each skipped anniversary, the official Info
   Sheet 571 percentage for that period, the 12-month elapse date, and the arithmetic. Retain
   the source printout.
3. Run and **document** the § 1942.5 retaliation screen for each unit, including a nil return
   where there is nothing to report.
4. Serve both units in the **same manner**, with the same notice template, within the same
   window — even though the effective dates differ.
5. Never reference a tenant's household composition, occupancy change, national origin,
   language, immigration status, source of income, familial status, age, disability, or any
   protected characteristic in any notice, email or contemporaneous note.
6. Do not use any pricing software that analyses competitor data. § 37.10C makes that unlawful
   and gives tenants and tenants' organisations a private right of action.
7. If a differential is nonetheless chosen — for example phasing Unit 2 at 10% while Unit 1
   takes 3.0% — record the reason at the time. "Household already paying above the ZIP median
   and 42.1% of ZIP renters are cost-burdened" is a documented, neutral, defensible reason.
   Silence is not.

**What this establishes.** The Outer Sunset is overwhelmingly pre-1979 stock — 93.7% — which
corroborates that a 1940 building sits inside Rent Ordinance coverage, and 64.2% of units date
from 1949 or earlier, consistent with the stated construction year. The ZIP's median gross rent
runs **13.7% above** the county median.

**Positioning of these two units.** Unit 1 at $3,995 is **41.9% above** the ZIP median and sits
in the top bracket where 30.7% of cash-rent households pay $3,500+. Unit 2 at $3,150 is **11.9%
above** the median; the recommended outcome of $3,562.65 would place it **26.5% above** the ZIP
median, inside the $3,500+ bracket.

**Why this matters to the decision.** It does **not** make either increase lawful or unlawful —
market position is irrelevant to the Rent Ordinance calculation, and no official SF source
treats comparability as a ground for an annual increase during a continuing tenancy. It matters
for two other reasons. First, **hardship exposure**: a tenant may seek relief from a
passthrough, and 42.1% cost-burden in the ZIP means a large increase lands on households with
little headroom. Second, **petition risk**: an increase that moves a household from below to
above the ZIP median is more likely to be challenged, and the challenge costs more than the
increase is worth. That is the practical case for the phased 10% option on Unit 2.

**Not reported, by design:** HUD Fair Market Rent and BLS CPI inputs. HUD's API returned
`Unauthenticated` and third-party FY2026 2-bedroom values conflicted ($3,604 vs $3,160); the
BLS regional release returned 404. Both are left blank — Flags **F-01** and **F-02**. The
operative published result (1.6%) is verified from sf.gov, and SF.gov instructs owners to use
the published percentage rather than recompute it.

First: do I need a Rent Board license as an individual owner?

Yes. The SF Rent Board says all owners of residential property must report through the Housing Inventory. For tenant-occupied units, the report generates a Rent Increase License; the rule is not limited to corporations. A current license must be on file before the rent increase takes effect. sf.gov — Housing Inventory

Separate filing vs. tenant notice: The official process is annual Housing Inventory reporting and then written notice to the tenant. The cited guidance does not direct an owner to file each individual rent-increase notice with the Rent Board, and the license itself does not need to be attached to the tenant notice. The license requirement is keyed to the increase's effective date—not expressly to the date the tenant notice is served. Prudent sequence: complete the Inventory, confirm the license in the Portal, then serve the tenant notice; in every case, make sure the license is current by the effective date. sf.gov — Housing Inventory; sf.gov — Rent increases

Bottom line: You do not get a special corporate exemption as an individual. Report/update the unit in the Housing Inventory and obtain the license; there is no separate Rent Board application for each annual increase described in the official guidance.

Bottom line

  • Annual allowable increase: 1.6% for rent-controlled units effective March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027. sf.gov — Current Rates
  • Unit 1: $3,995 × 1.6% = $63.92 per month; new rent $4,058.92. 1.6% is the entire current annual allowance, so it is the ceiling for any single increase; it can be imposed at most once every 12 months, and the increase date then becomes the anniversary date. April 1, 2026 is 14 months after the February 1, 2025 lease start, not an anniversary date — the official definition of the anniversary date is the day and month of the last increase, or of lease start when no increase was ever imposed. So 1.6% on 4/1/2026 was available in principle (more than 12 months had passed) but it resets the anniversary to April 1 and forfeits the 1.4%. sf.gov — formula and anniversary date; sf.gov — irregular periods
  • Unit 2: If no prior increases were imposed and the same tenants remain, the total turns on the disputed banking interval (see the flag below): 13.1% under the 12-month reading (11.5% banked + 1.6% current) = $3,150 × 13.1% = $412.65, new rent $3,562.65; or 11.7% under the conservative 24-month reading (10.1% banked + 1.6%) = $368.55, new rent $3,518.55. Both are arithmetic from an unverified current rent, and neither is a verified entitlement. sf.gov — 571 table; sf.gov — Banked increases
  • Notice: 30 days' written notice for 1.6%; 90 days' written notice for 13.1% because it exceeds 10%. Add 5 days if mailed. sf.gov; Civ. Code §827
  • License: A current rent increase license is required before an annual or banked increase takes effect. sf.gov — Housing Inventory
  • One unit or both: There is no official requirement to raise both units, and no official rule requires a uniform percentage. Each unit has its own base rent, anniversary date and rent history, so the lawful amounts are different by design. What matters legally is that each unit receives at most its own allowable amount for a documented, non-retaliatory, non-discriminatory reason. Applying one percentage to both is not a safe harbor: 1.6% on both would simply waive Unit 2's banked entitlement, and a percentage high enough to use Unit 2's banked total would be an unlawful increase on Unit 1. sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Banked increases
  • Discrimination: Fair housing laws prohibit treating tenants differently because of a protected characteristic. Raising both units is not a legal safe harbor; the key is a legitimate, documented, non-discriminatory reason. sf.gov — Housing discrimination; CRD — Housing; HUD — Report Housing Discrimination
  • Tenant-lawyer review: A tenant lawyer can review the calculation, notice, license, and documentation, but no one can guarantee approval in advance. sf.gov — Rent Board counselor

What is "reasonable and normal"?

The official standard for a rent-controlled unit is the Rent Board's annual allowable increase, not a market-rate or "reasonable rent" opinion. For 2026–27, that is 1.6%. sf.gov — Current Rates The annual allowable is intended to cover ordinary operating-cost increases; it is the normal, no-petition increase. sf.gov — Rent increases

Unit 2's 13.1% is not the "normal annual" amount. It is a one-time catch-up of skipped annual allowances (11.5%) plus the current annual allowance (1.6%). It is lawful only if the banked calculation is correct, the license is current, and the 90-day notice is given. sf.gov — Banked increases A tenant-friendly lawyer may scrutinize a seven-year catch-up even if it is technically lawful.

No official definition: Official sources do not define "reasonable" or "normal business practice" beyond the Rent Board formula. A tenant lawyer's approval cannot be guaranteed. sf.gov — Rent Board counselor

The five questions, answered

QuestionAnswerBasisOfficial source
1. What is a reasonable / normal increase under local, state and federal law?Local law supplies the only ceiling: 1.6% for 3/1/2026–2/28/2027, plus any lawfully banked percentages, once every 12 months. The state cap (5% + CPI, max 10%) does not raise this, because Civ. Code §1947.12(d)(3) exempts units covered by a stricter local ordinance; the Rent Board publishes the current state figure as 8.8% and says it does not apply to Rent Ordinance units. Federal law sets no rent ceiling at all — only anti-discrimination limits.Verifiedsf.gov — Current Rates; Civ. Code §1947.12(d)(3); sf.gov — AB 1482; HUD
2. How do large corporations handle increases vs. individual owners?The rule is identical for both; no official source creates a corporate rate or a separate process. Two verified structural differences: algorithmic/revenue-management rent-setting software is outright prohibited in San Francisco by Ordinance §37.10C, which in practice constrains larger operators; and the Costa-Hawkins / AB 1482 single-family and condo exemptions are unavailable to corporate owners. No official source publishes comparative behavioural data, so none is claimed here.Verified rule; practice claim not verifiablesf.gov — §37.10C; sf.gov — AB 1482
3. What is the correct way to notify tenants, and what reasons are reasonable?Written notice stating the dollar amount, percentage, effective date and the banked portion with the dates it is based on; 30 days if the increase is 10% or less, 90 days if more than 10%, plus 5 days if mailed. The annual allowable increase needs no reason at all — it is a statutory entitlement. Any above-cap increase requires a Rent Board petition filed before the notice.Verifiedsf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Banked increases; Civ. Code §827; sf.gov — Landlord petitions
4. Would a tenant lawyer find this reasonable?That cannot be guaranteed, and no official source defines "reasonable" beyond the formula. The test a tenant-side reviewer will actually apply is whether the arithmetic, the licence, the notice and the rent history are correct, and whether the increase is retaliatory under Ordinance §37.9(d) — if the tenant exercised a right within the six months before the increase, retaliation is presumed unless rebutted. Free representation is available to tenants, so assume the file will be examined.Verified standards; outcome not predictablesf.gov — §37.9(d); sf.gov — Tenant petitions; sf.gov — Tenant resources
5. Raise one unit or both?There is no legal requirement to raise both, and no legal safe harbour in doing so. The lawful amounts differ by unit by design (1.6% for Unit 1; 11.7–13.1% for Unit 2), so the defensible approach is one written, consistently applied policy that gives each unit its own lawful amount, with the calculation documented. A uniform percentage is not required and is either a waiver (if low) or unlawful (if high). Discrimination risk turns on protected characteristics and retaliation, not on whether both units were raised.Analysis; lawyer review advisedsf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Housing discrimination; CRD — Housing

How to read this page

This page exists so that nothing you asked is answered only in passing. Each row takes one question you asked, gives the answer the official sources support, states how firm that answer is, and links the source. It restates answers already published elsewhere on this site; it does not introduce a new percentage, dollar amount or deadline that is not derived by scripts/verify_calculations.py from the Rent Board’s published percentages and the statutory notice periods.

Status words used below. Verified means an official government, legislative or City source says it in terms, linked on the row. No source located means the question was searched and no official page answers it — that is a finding about the state of the sources, not an answer invented here.

1. The Rent Board question — asked first, answered first

Your questionAnswer from official sourcesStatusOfficial source
As an individual property owner, not a corporation, do I still need the Rent Board license? Yes. The Rent Board states that all owners of residential property are required to report into the Housing Inventory, and that property owners who report a unit as tenant-occupied receive a rent increase license. The text turns on the unit being rent-controlled and tenant-occupied, not on the owner’s corporate form. Rules § 13.14 grants the license to “a landlord who has substantially complied with their obligation to report information about a rental unit” — the trigger is reporting the unit. Verified — but the underlying coverage is unverified (F-01, F-31) sf.gov — Housing Inventory; Rules § 13.14
Does the under-ten-unit phase-in change that for a two-unit building? No. For condominium units and units in buildings with fewer than ten residential units, the reporting requirement began March 1, 2023 and the licence rule applies to increases effective on or after that date. Older start dates are long past; a current licence is required now either way. Verified Rent Ordinance § 37.15(c); Rules § 13.14; sf.gov — Housing Inventory
Do I have to apply for a separate licence for each increase? No separate licence application exists for an ordinary annual or banked increase. Reporting into the Housing Inventory automatically generates the licence for a non-owner-occupied unit. The Board’s pages describe two ongoing duties — annual Housing Inventory reporting (updated by March 1, and within 30 days of a change in business contact information) and a written notice to the tenant — and do not direct an owner to file each increase notice with the Board. Verified as a description of the official process sf.gov — Housing Inventory; § 37.15
Do I have to inform the Rent Board before notifying the tenants? Not on the date of the notice. Rules § 13.14 states the test in terms: “the effective date of the rent increase, not the date of service, shall be used to determine whether or not the increase conforms with the provisions of this section.” A licence obtained between service and the effective date is therefore sufficient on the face of the rule. Verifying the licence before serving is recommended on this site as prudence, not as a legal deadline. Verified (the rule); the sequencing advice is labelled prudence, not law Rules § 13.14; sf.gov — Housing Inventory
What happens if I raise the rent without the licence — is it just the increase that fails? The whole increase fails. An annual or banked increase “purporting to be effective while the landlord is unlicensed shall render the entire rent increase null and void”, and the Rent Board’s tenant page says a refund may be owed and that this “may apply for multiple years”. The same regulation adds that nothing in it affects the landlord’s banking rights — a void notice loses that increase, not the accumulated balance. Verified Rules § 13.14; sf.gov — Notice of Owner Noncompliance; § 37.3(b)(5)
What is the licence obligation in the ordinance itself? § 37.15(e): a landlord who has substantially complied with reporting “shall receive a license to impose rent increases”, and if the landlord has not, “the license to impose rent increases shall be temporarily suspended during the period of the landlord’s noncompliance”, with the tenant not obligated to pay the increase for the suspended months. § 37.3(a)(1) grants the annual increase only to a landlord who has reported under § 37.15, and puts banking under (a)(2) “subject to Section 37.15”. Verified — read verbatim from the ordinance text Rent Ordinance § 37.15; § 37.3(a)(1)–(2)
Is there any fee to get the licence? Reporting into the Housing Inventory is free. Separate from it, owners owe the annual Rent Board Fee — $59.00 per dwelling unit for 2026–27. Once paid in full, up to 50% ($29.50 per dwelling unit) may be recovered from tenants in occupancy on November 1. The exemption-request deadline for the 2027 fee is December 14, 2026, and payment is due March 1, 2027. The Board states that requesting an exemption does not satisfy Housing Inventory reporting. Verified; payment status for these units is unchecked (F-13) sf.gov — The Rent Board Fee; sf.gov — Housing Inventory
Is the Housing Inventory submission private? No. The Rent Board states that “the information entered in the housing inventory submission is public”, may include the owner’s name and business contact information, and is submitted under penalty of perjury. This site publishes none of it. Verified — recorded as F-21 sf.gov — Housing Inventory

2. What is a reasonable and normal increase for these two units?

Your questionAnswer from official sourcesStatusOfficial source
What is a reasonable and normal annual increase in ZIP 94122 under local law? 1.6% for increases effective March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027, calculated as 60% of the change in the Bay Area CPI and never more than 7%. The ordinance is citywide, so the ZIP code does not change the percentage. It applies once every 12 months. Verified sf.gov — Current Rates; Form 571 (26–27); § 37.3(a)(1)
Does the state cap (AB 1482) set a higher floor I could use? No. The Rent Board states the 8.8% AB 1482 maximum for San Francisco (August 1, 2026 – July 31, 2027) “does NOT apply for units under the rent increase limitations of the San Francisco Rent Ordinance”. Civil Code § 1947.12(d)(3) exempts housing subject to local rent control that restricts annual increases to less than the state figure. For units under San Francisco’s rent-increase limitations, the AB 1482 cap is not operative; Civil Code § 827 supplies notice mechanics independently. Verified sf.gov — AB 1482; Civ. Code § 1947.12
Is there a federal rent ceiling? No official federal source located in this review sets a dollar ceiling on a private, unsubsidised rent increase. What federal law does supply is an anti-discrimination rule: the Fair Housing Act prohibits different terms or conditions of rental on the basis of a protected class. That is the federal constraint on how the increase is chosen, not a percentage. No source located (recorded as F-34) — the absence is a finding, not a verified ceiling HUD — Report Housing Discrimination; 42 U.S.C. Chapter 45
So what can I actually take on Unit 1? On the supplied facts, the 2026-02-01 allowance became available at 1.4% and completes its 12-month wait on 2027-02-01. An increase on that date is 1.4% banked + 1.6% current = 3.0% = $119.85, new rent $4,114.85. Taking it earlier yields only 1.6% ($63.92 → $4,058.92) and the 1.4% is permanently lost. The 3.0% total is 10% or less, so the notice is 30 days (35 if mailed) only if other increases in the prior 12 months do not trigger the longer period. Arithmetic verified from published rates; base rent and tenancy date unverified (F-02) sf.gov — Rent increases (worked examples); sf.gov — Banked increases; Form 571
What can I actually take on Unit 2? On the supplied facts and assuming no increase was ever imposed since 2019-06-01, six allowances totalling 11.5% are banked, plus the 1.6% current allowance = 13.1% = $412.65, new rent $3,562.65. Because 13.1% exceeds 10%, the notice is 90 days (95 if mailed). Every part of this depends on the unverified rent history and the other legal gates, including F-28 for the off-anniversary use of banked amounts. A conservative annual-only alternative is 1.6% = $50.40 → $3,200.40, leaving 11.5% banked; because it uses no banked amount, F-28’s banked-timing issue does not arise. A phased 10.0% option is $315.00 → $3,465.00. A banked-only option on the 2027-06-01 anniversary is 11.5% = $362.25 → $3,512.25 with a 90-day notice (serve by 2027-03-03): it keeps the increase on the anniversary and at the time of an annual increase, takes no annual component, and needs no 2027–28 rate. Arithmetic verified; rent history unverified (F-06); effective date stored as a suggestion (F-28) sf.gov — Banked increases; Form 571; Civ. Code § 827(b)(3)(A)
Is 13.1% still “reasonable and normal”? It is a conditional arithmetic scenario, not a verified lawful total or the normal annual increase: it combines six skipped allowances with the current annual allowance. Coverage, licence, the complete ledger, retaliation/discrimination and F-28 still require review. No official source defines “reasonable” or “normal business practice” beyond the formula and the petition process, so this site does not claim a source for those words. No official definition located — F-09; the 13.1% calculation is conditional (F-06, F-28) sf.gov — Banked increases; sf.gov — Rent increases
Does the 1940 build year make the units rent-controlled? Almost, but the official test has two parts. A unit built before June 14, 1979 has rent control only if it is also not used as a single-family dwelling. For a two-unit building the second part is met, because the Rent Board states that a single-family dwelling with a legal or illegal in-law unit constitutes a two-unit building and is not exempt. Both the build year and the unit count are owner-supplied here. The test is verified; the build year and unit count are unverified (F-01, F-31) sf.gov — Learn about San Francisco rental laws; sf.gov — Costa-Hawkins partial exemption

3. How do corporations and individual owners handle increases?

Your questionAnswer from official sourcesStatusOfficial source
How do big real estate corporations handle rental increases in San Francisco? Official sources make no distinction. The Rent Ordinance regulates “landlords”, and the Housing Inventory applies to “all owners of residential property”. No official page located in this review sets out a separate corporate procedure for setting or noticing an increase. No source located for a distinct corporate practice — recorded as F-08 sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Housing Inventory
How do individual property owners handle them? Through the same four steps: report into the Housing Inventory (which generates the licence), calculate the allowable and any banked percentage on base rent, serve the written notice with the dollar amount, percentage, effective date and banked portion, and keep records. The Rent Board publishes the calculation examples and a free counselling line; it cannot give legal advice. Verified sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Banked increases; sf.gov — Speak with a Rent Board counselor
Does a corporation get more latitude on the percentage? No. The percentage comes from § 37.3 and the annual Rent Board announcement, and the licence, notice and anti-discrimination duties are the same. There is also a rule that applies to every landlord regardless of size: since October 14, 2024, § 37.10C prohibits selling or using algorithmic devices (“revenue management software”) that use non-public competitor data to set rents or occupancy levels, with each unit and each month a separate violation. Ordinary unit-management software is expressly not prohibited. Verified sf.gov — Algorithmic devices prohibited; § 37.3

4. The correct way to inform tenants, and legitimate reasons

Your questionAnswer from official sourcesStatusOfficial source
What is the correct way to inform tenants? A written notice served personally or by mail under Code of Civil Procedure § 1013, at least 30 days before the effective date if the increase is 10% or less, or 90 days if it is more than 10% (alone or combined with other increases in the 12 months before the effective date), plus 5 days if mailed. It must state the dollar amount, the percentage, the effective date and which part is banked. Verified Civ. Code § 827(b); sf.gov — Giving notice; sf.gov — Banked increases
Must the licence be attached to the notice? No. The Rent Board states the rent increase licence “does not need to be included with your rent increase notice” — but it must be current on the effective date. Verified sf.gov — Housing Inventory
What is a reasonable reason for the increase? For the annual and banked allowance, the Rent Board requires no separate business justification and no petition: the percentage is set by the Board and the duty is to calculate and notice it correctly. Anything above that needs a specific authorized basis: capital improvements, operating and maintenance, special circumstances, utility or bond passthroughs have distinct petition or worksheet rules. Rules § 6.14 / Costa-Hawkins is a separate roommate-history route for which a petition is optional, not required. No official source defines “reasonable” in general terms. Verified for the process; no official definition of “reasonable” (F-09) sf.gov — Landlord petitions and passthroughs; sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Landlord petitions; sf.gov — Rules 6.14 / Costa-Hawkins
Can I add rent because Unit 1 gained an occupant? No. § 37.3(a)(11)(A): a landlord may not impose increases solely because a tenant has added an occupant, “notwithstanding a rental agreement or lease that specifically permits a rent increase for additional occupants”. The Rent Board repeats it: this is an unlawful rent increase even if the lease provides for the charge. Verified § 37.3(a)(11); sf.gov — Additional occupants
Can Unit 1 be reset to market rent now that one original tenant left? Not on the supplied facts. Rules § 6.14 allows an unlimited increase only when the last original occupant vacates and a timely written 6.14 notice was served — generally within 60 days of the landlord knowing of the occupancy. An original tenant remains. Costa-Hawkins reaches a subtenant or assignee, never a co-tenant. Rule verified; the occupancy facts are unverified (F-05, F-15, F-20) sf.gov — Rules 6.14 and Costa-Hawkins
Is there anything that can bar an increase outright? Yes — retaliation. Civil Code § 1942.5 bars an increase within 180 days of the latest of a good-faith habitability notice or oral complaint, a recorded agency complaint, a resulting inspection or citation, a tenantability proceeding, or an adverse judgment; separately it bars an increase imposed to retaliate for lawful tenant organising. No complaint or repair history was supplied for either household, so this screen cannot be completed. The statutory bar is verified; the screen is unverified (F-18) Civ. Code § 1942.5

5. Would a tenant lawyer approve it — and should I raise one unit or both?

Your questionAnswer from official sourcesStatusOfficial source
Can I get a tenant lawyer to review and approve the increase? You can have one review it, and this site lists free and low-cost resources for doing so. What cannot be verified is approval: the Rent Board expressly states it “cannot give legal advice”, and a tenant may file an unlawful rent increase petition or a summary petition regardless of how the notice was prepared. No official source offers a pre-clearance or approval process for an annual or banked increase. Verified that no pre-clearance exists; approval cannot be guaranteed (F-10) sf.gov — Speak with a Rent Board counselor; sf.gov — Tenant Petitions; sf.gov — Tenant and Landlord Resources
What will such a reviewer check first? Coverage; whether a current licence was on file on the effective date; whether the base rent and anniversary date are right; whether the percentage is the one in effect on the effective date and whether each banked allowance completed its 12-month wait without compounding or rounding up; whether the notice period and content comply with § 827; and whether any protected-class discrimination or retaliation is present. Each element verified; whether this file passes is unverified until reviewed Rules § 13.14; sf.gov — Banked increases; Civ. Code § 827; sf.gov — Housing discrimination
Is it OK to raise the rent on only one unit? Yes. Nothing in the Rent Ordinance requires simultaneous increases, and each unit’s lawful amount is driven by its own base rent and rent history — so the two units legitimately produce different percentages. Unit 1’s maximum at the current allowance is 1.6% (or 3.0% on its 2027-02-01 anniversary); Unit 2’s 13.1% is conditional on the ledger, other legal gates and F-28; its annual-only scenario is 1.6% ($50.40 → $3,200.40), leaving 11.5% banked. Applying 13.1% to both would exceed Unit 1’s calculated allowance, while applying only 1.6% to both is a permissible conservative scenario, not a requirement. Verified that no such requirement exists; the amounts are unverified as to inputs sf.gov — Rent increases; sf.gov — Banked increases
Would raising both units avoid a discrimination claim? Not by itself. Federal, state and local fair housing law prohibit different terms or conditions of rental because of a protected characteristic. They do not require identical percentages, and matching the two numbers is not a safe harbour — raising both by 13.1% would simply make Unit 1 unlawful. A documented, neutral, unit-specific reason is the relevant practice; if verified, the different rent histories can supply that explanation. They are owner-supplied here and do not create a safe harbour. Verified as to the legal standard; no official source endorses a “raise both” strategy HUD — Report Housing Discrimination; CRD — Housing; sf.gov — Housing discrimination
What is the single most likely way this increase gets challenged? Two, both visible in the official materials. First, the licence: a tenant can search the Portal for a licence on the effective date and file an unlawful rent increase petition if none existed, with a possible multi-year refund. Second, the arithmetic and the ledger: a tenant can petition for a determination of lawful rent, and the landlord bears the burden of proving the calculation. Both routes are free to the tenant. Verified sf.gov — Notice of Owner Noncompliance; sf.gov — Tenant Petitions
Does anything change if the pending File No. 261020 passes? It could. The Board of Supervisors is considering File No. 261020, which would add § 37.3(h) capping banked and capital-improvement increases at 10% of the lowest base rent in the prior 12 months, applying to banked notices given on or after September 15, 2026. Status is Pending Committee Action — not enacted. The proposed cap is not in force, but Unit 2’s 13.1% remains conditional on every other gate and F-28. The phased 10.0% option ($315.00 → $3,465.00) does not exceed either reading of the draft, subject to all other legal conditions. Re-check Legistar before serving. Status verified 2026-11-11; the outcome is unknowable — recorded as F-27 Legistar — File 261020; Leg Ver1; sf.gov — Current Rates (notice 2026-11-09)

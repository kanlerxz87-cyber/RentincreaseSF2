Owner Practices

How large corporate owners and individual owners handle increases, based only on verified statutory differences.

A limitation stated up front — Flag F-21 No official government source was found that quantifies how large corporate landlords set rent increases differently from individual owners in San Francisco. Any statement of the form 'corporations typically raise rent by X%' or 'individual owners usually do Y' would be unsourced. The Corporate vs Individual Owner page answers the question STRUCTURALLY instead — using the verified statutory differences in how the law treats corporate and individual owners (Costa-Hawkins and AB 1482 entity disqualifications, the § 37.10C algorithmic-pricing ban, Housing Inventory obligations) — and expressly declines to publish any unverifiable behavioural statistic.

The headline finding

The percentage is identical for both kinds of owner. The Rent Board publishes one allowable increase — currently 1.6% — and it applies to every covered unit in the city regardless of who owns it. There is no corporate rate and no small-owner rate. What differs is legal exposure and administrative discipline.

What this means for an individual owner

In San Francisco the corporate playbook of algorithmic, competitor-data-driven pricing is illegal, not merely frowned upon. That is a hard legal prohibition with a private right of action for tenants and tenants’ organisations, enacted in October 2024. So the question “how do the big companies do it?” has a partly surprising answer: the most common large-portfolio technique for setting rent levels is banned outright in this city, and using it exposes the owner to per-unit, per-month penalties.

What actually separates a large owner from an individual owner is therefore administrative discipline, not a bigger lawful increase:

  • Calendar each unit’s anniversary date and impose the annual increase every year.
  • File the Housing Inventory report every March 1 and confirm the license is current before any effective date.
  • Serve 30-day written notice on time, using the percentage in effect on the effective date.
  • Keep a computation sheet per unit per year.
The defensible practice is the boring one. Take the annual allowable increase every year, on the anniversary date, by 30-day written notice, with the license current, the arithmetic documented, and a neutral reason on the record. That is normal business practice, it is easy to defend, and it never reaches the 90-day-notice threshold. The owner who does this never has to attempt a 13.1% catch-up and never has to explain one.
